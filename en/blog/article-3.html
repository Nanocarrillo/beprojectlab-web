Ownership doesn't stop with the leader

Sep 25, 2026 · 4 min read

I used to think ownership was a leadership trait, something you either had or didn't as the person in charge. Managing teams across Latin America and Europe changed my mind. The pattern that showed up in every project that actually succeeded wasn't about my ownership. It was about whether the people doing the work felt it too.

So the question stopped being how much ownership I had, and became what I was doing to make it possible for anyone else.

Start with fit, not with the ask

You can't build that feeling out of nothing. Before you can ask someone to own a result, they have to be on a task that matches their skills and their capacity. Ownership placed on the wrong task doesn't produce commitment, it produces frustration, and the person ends up carrying the weight of something they were never set up to carry.

That part is uncomfortable, because it puts the first move on the leader rather than on the person's attitude. If the fit is wrong, no amount of asking fixes it.

Then get out of the way

Once someone is in the right role, you have to let them work. No micromanaging. Ownership can't take root under constant supervision, because supervision quietly tells someone the result isn't really theirs, it's yours, and they're just executing it.

The same applies to mistakes. Honest errors have to be survivable, or people stop making the kind of decisions that ownership requires in the first place. The literature calls this psychological safety, and without it ownership curdles into defensiveness instead of responsibility.

What you can delegate and what you can't

Here's the tension underneath both of those. You can delegate a task. You can delegate authority. What you can't delegate is accountability.

As the leader, you remain the global owner of the project's results, even when every individual result along the way belongs to someone else. This isn't a contradiction, it's a structure: responsibility nested at different levels rather than responsibility diluted as it spreads. Each person is accountable to you for their piece. You're accountable, upward, for all of it.

There's a name for the feeling I kept seeing on the ground. Psychological ownership, a concept from organizational behavior research associated with Pierce and his colleagues, describes how people can feel a task or an outcome belongs to them without any formal or legal claim to it, and how that feeling is what drives them past the minimum. My job wasn't to demand that feeling. It was to create the conditions where it could grow.

When someone isn't getting there

Sometimes those conditions include a hard conversation. When someone isn't reaching the result you expected, the instinct is to solve it for them, which is the fastest way to take the ownership back.

The better move is the one behind the GROW model, Goal, Reality, Options, Will: walking someone through their own situation with questions instead of answers, so the thinking, and the ownership of the fix, stays theirs.

The shadow side nobody warns you about

Strong individual ownership can tip into territory. When everyone owns their piece fiercely, collaboration across roles starts to erode, and people protect their slice instead of connecting it to everyone else's.

So the leader's job doesn't end at building ownership. It extends to keeping communication open across roles and departments even as individual accountability sharpens. Ownership that calcifies into silos costs more than the commitment it bought you.

Where it left me

I stopped thinking of ownership as something a leader has, and started seeing it as something a leader builds conditions for: the right fit, room to work, honest mistakes absorbed, and accountability that stays mine no matter how widely the work is spread.

Which brings me back to where this series started. The first two articles looked outward, at a supply chain with a single point of failure and at a client relationship tangled with ownership itself, and at how a leader builds a picture and holds enough trust together to move while the path is still unclear. This one turns the lens inward.

Once you accept that no leader can map the unknown alone, the real constraint becomes whether your team can act inside that same uncertainty alongside you. That only happens if ownership is distributed widely enough that people can move without waiting for certainty to come down from the top.

If you're leading something without a clear path ahead, the useful question isn't how much of it you can carry. It's how much of it the people around you are actually able to carry with you.

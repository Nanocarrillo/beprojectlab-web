Shaping the Unknown · Article 2

One point of contact, three pillars, and the fight to keep product first

Sep 12, 2026 · 4 min read

I was handed a relationship that was falling apart from the inside. I was the account manager for a software product, and our biggest client was also our parent company. The owner and the customer were the same entity, and that entanglement was already doing damage before I touched anything.

The parent company treated its own requests as automatically the highest priority in our development pipeline, whether or not they were what the product actually needed. Communication was scattered across our CEO, our project lead, the CTO, and our product owner, each talking to the parent company separately, with no single channel. The result was a team with a bad atmosphere, worn down by constant friction with no clear source.

Start with a picture, not a fix

Before building anything, I spent two to three weeks just listening, separately, to both sides. I talked to my own team about where they felt blocked and what was actually breaking. Then I went to the parent company's offices and talked to the people actually using the product, not just the person managing the relationship.

That's what let me see the real shape of the problem. It wasn't one issue, it was three tangled together: no single point of contact, no shared framework for how these joint projects should run, and no agreed answer to whose priorities the roadmap actually served. Treating it as a communication problem alone would have fixed the symptom and left the rest untouched.

Three pillars, not one fix

Out of that listening came a plan built on three pillars.

The first was communication, made deliberately narrow: a single channel and fixed meetings, replacing the fragmented free-for-all where everyone on our side talked to the parent company on their own.

The second was structural: building a specific project management framework for exactly this kind of joint development, something that didn't exist before and had been improvised project by project.

The third was about where decisions were anchored. Product choices had to stay tied to what was actually right for our product, not to whichever request from the parent company landed with the most pressure behind it. Product-led, not stakeholder-led.

I was named the single point of contact between both companies, which is what made pillar one enforceable in practice, not just on paper.

What the plan didn't solve by itself

On paper, three pillars and one point of contact sounds like it closes the loop. It doesn't, not entirely. A framework can specify how priorities get decided. It can't make a powerful stakeholder feel good about not always winning. A single channel can stop the fragmentation. It can't, on its own, make both sides trust that the person running that channel is reading the situation correctly.

That gap is where something else had to carry the weight.

The pillars needed something underneath them

Here's what I noticed looking back: the three pillars only worked because something else was holding them up, something that isn't a fourth pillar so much as the ground the other three stood on. Call it confidence in the leadership.

You can design the best framework in the world. You can define the cleanest communication channel. You can be as disciplined as possible about staying product-led. But the project itself was still, in a real sense, unknown territory, an entangled owner-client relationship with no established playbook. Structure alone doesn't resolve that. What resolves it is both companies having enough confidence in the person holding the structure together, and in the procedures he'd defined, to keep acting even while the outcome was still unwritten.

There's a name for this in the literature. Karl Weick's idea of sensemaking captures it well: under uncertainty, people don't need their leader to have a perfect map of the territory. They need enough confidence in how the leader is reading the situation to keep moving anyway. Sensemaking isn't about predicting correctly, it's about narrating the ambiguity clearly enough that the group can keep acting inside it.

Ronald Heifetz's concept of the holding environment, from his work on adaptive leadership, points at the same thing from another angle. The leader's job, in part, is to absorb the anxiety that ambiguity produces, so that the team, and in this case the parent company too, can trust the structure even while the final result is still unknown.

Where it left me

The fragmented relationship is gone. In its place: one channel, one framework, and a roadmap anchored to the product instead of to whoever pushed hardest.

But if I'm honest about what actually held that together while it was still being built, it wasn't the framework and it wasn't the meeting cadence. Those were necessary. What was sufficient was that both sides had come to trust how I was reading a situation neither of us had a map for.

If you're building structure into a relationship that's still finding its shape, the structure matters. But don't mistake it for the whole answer. Somebody still has to be trusted to read the room while the map is being drawn.

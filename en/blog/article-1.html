What I learned rebuilding a supply chain with a single point of failure

Sep 5, 2026 · 5 min read

I was handed a supply chain where one supplier covered our main market. If that supplier failed for any reason — a late shipment, a shutdown, a dispute over terms — we had nothing behind it. My job was to fix that.

My first reaction was to go find a second supplier and close the gap. I didn't.

Start with a picture, not a number

Before looking at anyone, we defined what the new chain had to look like.

Not only the numbers. Cost and lead time were the easy part. The harder part was the rest: what kind of relationship we needed with each supplier, how much trust we had to build before handing over real volume, how the whole setup should respond the day something went wrong. Those are hard to measure, so they usually get left out. Leave them out and you get a plan that looks solid on a spreadsheet and falls apart under normal pressure.

Only after that did the search start. We added four new suppliers in four different markets — five in total, spread across the globe. A network instead of a single point.

The plan bends, the picture holds

On paper, getting there was a straight line: define the profile, select the suppliers, sign, go live. It wasn't. Some milestones landed on time. Others slipped.

The plan changed more than once. The picture behind it didn't. That's the part worth noticing — what kept the project on course wasn't only hitting every date (when possible), it was that the picture stayed clear enough to steer by while the schedule bent around it. Mintzberg called this the tension between deliberate and emergent strategy: no real project is fully planned or fully improvised.

That doesn't make the picture permanent. But there's a difference between the two things that can make you revise it. A missed date isn't a reason to redraw the destination — that's the plan absorbing reality, which is what plans are for. What justifies redrawing it is the world it describes actually moving: the market changes, the requirement changes, what "good" looks like changes. The first is noise. The second is a reason. Confuse them and you either keep chasing a picture that stopped being true, or you redraw it every time a date slips and end up with no fixed reference at all.

The shortage moved one risk, not the other

A shortage on the business supply chain appeared and it had nothing to do with the project. AI drove demand through the roof and it hit the whole market. But it landed while the new chain was still being built, and that's what made it matter.

The project's risk didn't change. The plan was still the plan.

The business's risk did. The four new suppliers weren't live yet, so the company was still running on one — the exact exposure the project existed to remove. The thing we were protecting against had just become more likely, and the protection wasn't installed.

So the pressure to move faster wasn't about being behind schedule. It was about how long we stayed exposed.

That's where two lines have to be drawn, and they're not the same line.

One is whether it can be calculated. That's Frank Knight's distinction: risk is what you can put a number on, uncertainty is what you can't. The shortage had no number.

The other is where it comes from. Exogenous uncertainty comes from outside and doesn't move with your decisions. Endogenous comes from inside — your own choices create it, and your own choices can reduce it. The shortage was exogenous. The exposure window — how long the old setup stayed the only setup — was endogenous, and it was the only place where pushing harder did anything.

So that's where we pushed. And it worked — the chain came up faster than it otherwise would have. Not because we handled the shortage. We didn't touch the shortage. We just spent less time standing in front of it.

Discipline is my strength. That's why I had to be precise about it

Discipline is what I'm good at. It's what I bring to a project. Which is exactly why it was worth being honest about what it does and what it doesn't.

Planning is what lowered our risk. Buffers, five suppliers across a global network instead of one, clear thresholds for when a supplier gets replaced. That's what brought the number down on paper.

Discipline doesn't lower risk. What it does is keep the risk on paper and the real risk from drifting apart. A mitigation plan nobody follows consistently stops protecting anyone, even though the document still says the risk is handled. A disciplined team executing a weak plan is exposed exactly like a team doing nothing. Discipline doesn't fix a bad plan — it stops a good one from quietly eroding.

That distinction has a practical use. When something goes wrong, it tells you which question to ask first: is the plan bad, or is the plan not being executed? Those need opposite responses. Pushing harder on execution when the plan itself is the problem just burns the team out.

It's also not one thing. A leader's discipline gets tested when everyone else starts doubting the plan after a visible miss — the job there isn't to have the answer, it's to hold the course long enough that doubt doesn't turn into paralysis. A team's discipline is the daily follow-through, with or without supervision, that turns a plan into actual progress.

We saw both when the shortage hit. The suppliers still on schedule kept moving exactly as planned while the delayed one was worked through. Nobody had to be told. And notice what discipline was doing there: it wasn't solving the shortage. Nothing on my side could solve the shortage. It was keeping everything else from unravelling while something outside our control ran its course.

Where it left me

The single point of failure is gone. In its place, five suppliers across a global network. What replaced it wasn't certainty — the new markets are still new, and the component market proved it can move faster than anyone's plan.

What replaced it was a picture clear enough to steer by, and enough discipline to keep steering when the road stopped being straight.

If you're starting something without a clear path ahead, the useful question isn't how to remove the uncertainty. It's which part of it is actually yours to reduce, and which part you just have to stay light enough on your feet to survive.
